WHAT IS INQUIRY-BASED TEACHING?
Learners investigate a question, gather and interpret information, explain their
reasoning, and draw conclusions. The teacher designs the question and guides the
process, but learners do the thinking.

THE INQUIRY CYCLE
1. Notice - What do we see or wonder?
2. Question - What do we want to find out?
3. Investigate - How can we find out?
4. Explain - What do the findings mean?
5. Apply - How can we use this idea?
6. Reflect - What new questions do we have?

DESIGNING A GOOD INQUIRY QUESTION
Strong questions:
- have more than one possible route to an answer,
- connect to learners' lives,
- can be investigated with available materials,
- require evidence or reasoning.

Examples:
  Weak: "What is evaporation?"
  Strong: "Why does a wet cloth dry faster in the sun than in the shade?"

  Weak: "List the parts of a plant."
  Strong: "How does removing leaves affect a plant over one week?"

  Weak: "What is a fraction?"
  Strong: "How can we share three chapati equally among four learners?"

SIMPLE INVESTIGATIONS WITH LOCAL MATERIALS
- Water and containers: absorption, floating, evaporation, capacity.
- Seeds and soil: conditions for growth.
- Paper and string: strength, balance, measurement.
- Stories and interviews: community history and local knowledge.
- Sorting objects: classification, patterns, and comparison.

ROLES DURING INQUIRY
Teacher:
  asks open questions, listens, observes, and keeps the investigation safe.
Learners:
  predict, test, record, discuss, and explain.
Groups:
  share roles such as materials manager, recorder, reporter, and timekeeper.

RECORDING FINDINGS
Use a simple table:
  What we changed | What we measured | What we noticed | What we think
Ask learners to write one sentence of explanation using "because".

TALK MOVES THAT DEEPEN THINKING
  "What makes you say that?"
  "Can you find evidence for that?"
  "Does anyone have a different idea?"
  "What would happen if we changed one thing?"
  "How could we test that?"

ASSESSING INQUIRY
Look for:
- a clear prediction,
- careful observation,
- use of evidence,
- willingness to revise an idea,
- clear explanation to others.
A learner can be right about the result but still need support in explaining how
they know.

COMMON PITFALLS
- Asking a question that has one expected answer.
- Giving learners the conclusion before they investigate.
- Skipping the discussion and going straight to the worksheet.
- Leaving no time for reflection.
- Using materials that are not safe or not available.
